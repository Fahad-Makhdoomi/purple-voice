Meta's Muse assistant can now call businesses on behalf of paid subscribers in the US, Meta's chief AI officer Alexandr Wang announced on X on November 12, 2026. The move to let Meta Muse call businesses comes a week after Meta opened Muse Voice, its real-time voice and video chat mode, to everyone in the US.

For anyone who answers a business phone, the practical upshot is simple: more of the callers on the other end will be software working for a customer.

## What Meta launched

There are two separate releases here, and they are easy to blur together.

**Muse Voice (November 5).** According to TechCrunch, Muse Voice lets users talk and video chat with Muse in real time, with an animated avatar that reacts to the tone of the conversation. It runs on mobile, web and Mac in the US, and Meta says it will come to its AI glasses and a planned "Muse Charm" wearable later. Meta first showed the feature at its Connect conference in September.

**Outbound calls to businesses (November 12).** According to Android Authority, Wang said Muse can now make outgoing calls for paid US subscribers. Paid plans reportedly start at $20 a month, with a $100 tier for heavier use. The user gives Muse a task that needs a phone call, such as booking a table or asking a customer service question, and Muse holds the conversation. Reported examples include restaurants, insurers and cable companies.

| Release | Date | Who gets it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Muse Voice (voice and video chat) | Nov 5, 2026 | Everyone in the US |
| Muse calls to businesses | Nov 12, 2026 | Paid subscribers in the US |

The calling feature has been in limited testing since September. That testing drew scrutiny: Reuters and 404 Media reported that Meta had human contractors quietly handle some calls, and Reuters reported Meta paused that test after privacy concerns. 404 Media also reported that some businesses hung up once they realised an AI was calling. Meta has not said publicly how often calls in the paid rollout fail or get escalated.

Meta is not alone. Android Authority points to Google's Call for Me on eligible Pixel phones and calling features from other assistants and startups.

## Why it matters for teams that sell or support by phone

Most writing about AI calling looks at businesses placing calls. This launch flips the direction. A consumer assistant with a large user base is now dialling businesses, which means receptionists, sales desks and support lines will field AI callers whether they planned for it or not.

A few practical questions to settle now:

- **What will you confirm to an AI caller?** Opening hours and availability are low risk. Account changes, payments or anything that needs identity checks probably need the customer on the line.
- **How do bookings get verified?** If Muse books a slot, send the confirmation to the customer directly, not just to the caller.
- **Does your own phone tree cope?** IVR menus and hold queues were built for people. If your inbound line is hard for a human to navigate, an AI caller may give up or misroute. Our comparison of [AI voice agents vs IVR](/blog/ai-voice-agent-vs-ivr) covers where menus break down.
- **Will staff hang up?** Some already do. Decide whether that is policy or habit.

There is a second-order point for teams running their own AI calling. Public attention on whether callers are human or AI is rising, partly because of the contractor story above. If your agent calls leads, say clearly that it is an AI assistant. Our guide on [how to disclose AI on calls](/blog/disclose-ai-on-calls) has wording that works.

## What to watch next

- Whether Meta publishes rules for how Muse identifies itself on calls and what it does when a business asks for a human.
- Expansion beyond the US, which Meta has not announced for calling.
- How businesses respond: some may add AI-specific handling to their inbound lines, others may block or screen AI callers.
- Whether consumer AI calling pushes regulators to look again at AI voice rules, which today focus mostly on businesses calling consumers.
