Helix, a Los Angeles voice biometrics startup, has raised a $6.2 million pre-seed round led by Chicago Ventures, according to reports published November 12, 2026. The company sells on-device voice checks that aim to tell a live person from a bot and estimate whether a speaker is an adult or a child. BAM Ventures, Aglaé Ventures, Corazon Capital, Tusk Ventures, Yonder Ventures, Rule30, Press On Ventures and Brown Angel Group also invested.

## What Helix announced

Helix was founded in 2025 by Alex Oberg, Anna Dorofiyenko and Mike Hall. Oberg previously worked on Ticketmaster's Verified Fan program, which was built to keep bots from buying tickets. Siri co-founder Dag Kittlaus is an adviser, and former Ticketmaster president Jared Smith sits on the board.

The product works like this, according to the company:

1. A user speaks a short four-digit challenge.
2. Software on the user's phone checks whether the voice is live human speech rather than synthetic or replayed audio.
3. It also estimates the speaker's age.
4. Helix says the whole check takes under four seconds and that no biometric data or other personal information is stored on its servers.

Helix sells this as one SDK covering bot management, biometric multi-factor authentication and age assurance. It is pitching ticketing, dating, gaming, gambling and fintech platforms. The company says it is in beta with early partners, including in-home wellness platform Zeel, which is evaluating Helix as an extra verification step.

Helix also says it meets the ISO/IEC 27566-1:2025 age assurance standard, is accredited under the Age Check Certification Scheme, and is among the first companies certified under an IEEE online age verification program. Helix links that program to New York's SAFE for Kids Act, which it says takes effect in January 2027. These are company statements; check current rules for your own situation, and treat this as general information rather than legal advice.

## Why it matters for teams that sell or support by phone

Helix is not a phone product today. Its launch describes an app and web SDK, not a tool for call centers. But the problem it targets is the same one creeping into phone work: as synthetic voices get better, it gets harder to know who, or what, is on the other end of a line.

That cuts both ways for sales and support teams:

- **Inbound fraud risk rises.** Cloned voices can be used to impersonate customers. Teams that verify callers by voice alone will need stronger checks.
- **Outbound trust gets harder to earn.** If people come to expect bots on every call, an AI agent that hides what it is will lose trust fast. Clear disclosure at the start of a call is the simplest defence. Our guide on [disclosing AI on calls](/blog/disclose-ai-on-calls) covers how to phrase it.
- **Rules are moving.** Several jurisdictions already set conditions on AI-generated voices in calls. See our overview of [TCPA and AI voice calls](/blog/tcpa-and-ai-voice-calls) for the US picture.

The broader point is that verification is becoming its own layer of the voice stack. Funding for companies like Helix suggests investors expect that layer to sit next to every voice channel eventually, including the phone.

## What to watch next

- **Independent accuracy testing.** Helix's performance figures are company-reported. Third-party results will matter more than launch claims, especially on the newest voice cloning tools.
- **Expansion beyond apps.** If Helix or similar companies move into telephony, contact centers could gain a fast way to check that a caller is a live person.
- **Age assurance deadlines.** New York's rules, as described by Helix, could push more platforms to adopt voice-based checks in 2027.
