AI voice cloning scams were the subject of three separate stories in the first week of November 2026. The Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department warned residents about AI-generated calls imitating loved ones, a Kansas City TV station showed a reporter's voice could be cloned for $11, and federal prosecutors in Manhattan unsealed charges against three men accused of using AI to impersonate executives in a scheme that cost companies about $129 million.

## What happened

**A sheriff's warning in Los Angeles.** On November 4, the Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department posted a video on Facebook warning about a recent wave of AI-generated calls, KTLA reported. The department said scammers are imitating voices, producing realistic video and sending convincing messages that appear to come from people the victim knows, usually claiming an emergency to get money. Its advice: pause, hang up, and contact the person on a number you already have.

**An $11 clone in Kansas City.** For its "Scam School" series, KMBC anchor Brya Berry worked with a cybersecurity expert from Tenfold Security to clone her voice from about 32 minutes of video she had already posted on YouTube and Facebook. The software cost $11. Using the clone, she called her cousin, who believed she was talking to Berry, though she noticed some pauses. No studio recording or script was needed.

**Federal charges in New York.** On November 6, prosecutors in the Southern District of New York unsealed an indictment against Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan and Guy Sheetrit. According to the ABA Journal and other reports, prosecutors allege the men used AI-manipulated voices and images to pose as company executives asking staff for help with a confidential deal, then posed as lawyers at a major law firm to direct wire transfers. The alleged scheme ran from 2024 into 2026. Reported losses include:

- a U.K. technology company, more than $11 million
- a Florida pet food company, about $5 million
- a Missouri hospital system, nearly $5 million
- a Massachusetts mutual fund, more than $4.6 million

The charges include wire fraud, money laundering and identity theft. They are allegations, and the defendants are presumed innocent unless proven guilty.

## Why it matters for teams that sell or support by phone

The common thread is that a familiar-sounding voice is no longer evidence of who is speaking. That has two consequences for businesses.

First, internal controls. The New York case targeted finance staff, not consumers. Any process that lets a phone call alone authorise money movement is exposed. Practical steps:

- Require a callback to a number on file before acting on any payment, bank-detail change or "confidential" request.
- Use a second channel (a ticket, an approval in your finance system) for anything over a set amount.
- Tell staff explicitly that urgency and secrecy are warning signs, whoever seems to be asking.

Second, outbound calling. As more people learn to distrust voices, legitimate AI callers have to make themselves easy to verify. That means saying up front that the caller is an AI agent, naming the business, explaining why you are calling, and never asking for payment details or passwords on an unscheduled outbound call. It also helps to give people a way to verify you on their own terms, such as a number listed on your website. ## What to watch next

- Extradition and court proceedings in the New York case, which will test the prosecutors' allegations.
- Further warnings from local law enforcement, which are useful signals of the scripts scammers are currently using.
- Whether regulators push further on disclosure rules for AI calls, which would raise the bar for every business using them.
