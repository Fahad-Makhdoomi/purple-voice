AI calling for insurance is most useful on the repetitive, time-bound calls: following up a quote request, reminding someone their policy renews next month, and booking a review with a licensed adviser. It is not a substitute for advice. Selling and recommending cover is a regulated job, so the sensible setup keeps the AI on qualification and scheduling, and hands anything that sounds like advice to a person.

## Where insurance teams lose leads and renewals

Insurance has a particular shape. Leads are expensive to acquire, quotes are easy to ignore, and renewals arrive on a fixed date whether your team is ready or not.

Common leaks in an agency or brokerage:

- A person requests a quote online and no one calls for two days.
- The quote goes out by email and sits unread.
- Renewal reminders are sent in a batch and not followed up.
- Advisers spend mornings making "just checking in" calls that rarely lead anywhere.

These are good targets for automation because each call has a clear, narrow goal.

## Use case 1: quote follow-up calls

After a quote is sent, an agent can phone and run a short, friendly check-in. A reasonable flow:

1. Introduce itself as an AI assistant calling for your agency.
2. Confirm they requested a quote and that they received it.
3. Ask if they have questions the team can answer, and answer basic ones from the knowledge base, such as how to pay or how to contact the claims line.
4. Ask where they are in the decision: comparing, ready to go ahead, or not interested.
5. Offer to book a call with an adviser if they want to proceed or need detail.

The key discipline is what the agent does not do. It should not explain exclusions, compare to a competitor's quote or suggest changes to cover. Write that into the prompt as a firm guardrail. Our guide to [writing an AI voice agent prompt](/blog/how-to-write-an-ai-voice-agent-prompt) covers how to phrase guardrails so they hold up in a real call.

## Use case 2: renewal calls

Renewals are where retention is won. A renewal call can:

- Confirm the policyholder still lives at the address or still runs the same business.
- Ask whether anything has changed, such as a new vehicle, an extension or a new driver.
- Flag that the renewal date is approaching.
- Book a review call for anyone whose circumstances have changed or who wants to shop around.

Because these calls are to existing customers, the tone is different from a cold call. The prompt should use the customer's name, mention their policy type in general terms, and keep the call under two minutes. Anyone who says something that suggests a change in risk, such as "we have just had a baby" or "I started a side business", should be flagged for a human and not processed further by the AI.

## Use case 3: inbound after-hours and overflow

An agent can also pick up inbound calls out of hours. It can take details for a quote request, answer questions about opening times and claims contact numbers, and book a callback. Do not let it take claim notifications unless you have designed that flow carefully with your compliance team. A first notice of loss is sensitive, and mistakes are costly.

## What to ask in qualification

A short set of qualifying questions helps your advisers prioritise. For a personal-lines lead you might gather:

| Detail | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Type of cover | Routes to the right adviser |
| Current renewal date | Tells you how urgent the lead is |
| Reason for looking | Price, service, a life change |
| Preferred contact time | Improves your next call |
| Whether they want a human call | Respects preference |

Resist the urge to collect full risk details by AI. Those are better handled by a licensed adviser using your underwriting forms. For more on structuring questions, read [AI lead qualification calls](/blog/ai-lead-qualification-calls).

## What to log and keep

After each call, you should have:

- A summary of what was said, in a few lines.
- A transcript and, if you record, the recording.
- The outcome: booked, call back later, not interested, do not call.
- A note of any statement that needs human review.

## Compliance and disclosure

Insurance is regulated in nearly every market, and telemarketing rules sit on top of that. In the US, the TCPA and state rules apply to calls using artificial or prerecorded voices. In the UK, PECR and FCA rules apply. Elsewhere, local rules differ. This is general information, not legal advice, so take your own advice before launching.

Practical habits that help in any jurisdiction:

- Tell the person early that they are speaking to an AI assistant.
- Only call people who have a clear connection to your business, such as a quote request or an existing policy.
- Honour do-not-call requests immediately and record them.
- Keep call times inside permitted hours.
- Never make the AI pretend to hold a licence.

See [disclosing AI on calls](/blog/disclose-ai-on-calls) and [TCPA and AI voice calls](/blog/tcpa-and-ai-voice-calls) for more.

## Keep humans where it counts

A good split is simple. The AI handles: first contact, reminders, scheduling, FAQs about logistics. Licensed staff handle: advice, recommendations, quotes that depend on underwriting judgement, claims, complaints and vulnerable customers.

If a caller sounds distressed or confused, the agent should offer a human and end the AI part of the conversation. A transfer to a live adviser during working hours, with a short briefing, is far better than pushing on. Our piece on [warm transfers](/blog/warm-transfer-ai-to-human) explains how to set this up.

## A note on tone with policyholders

Insurance calls often reach people at stressful moments: a price rise, a life change, a recent claim. Write the prompt so the agent is unhurried, uses plain language and avoids jargon such as "excess" or "indemnity" unless the caller uses it first. Tell it to apologise briefly if a caller is annoyed and to offer a human straight away rather than defending the process. Keep calls short, and never let the agent imply urgency that does not exist. A renewal date is a fact; "you must act today" is pressure. Reviewing a handful of transcripts each week will show you quickly where the tone slips.
