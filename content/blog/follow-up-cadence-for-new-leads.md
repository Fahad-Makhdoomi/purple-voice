A good lead follow-up cadence is a planned mix of calls, texts and emails, spaced so the early touches come fast and the later ones slow down, with a clear point at which you stop. The aim is to be easy to reach and hard to ignore, without being a nuisance.

Most teams lose leads not because the first call went badly but because the second, third and fourth touches never happen. A written cadence fixes that, and an AI voice agent can run the call steps consistently. This article gives a seven-touch example, the reasoning behind it, and the rules for stopping.

## Why a cadence beats chasing

Without a plan, follow-up depends on who remembers, who is busy and who is feeling optimistic. Leads get one call, then nothing for a week, then three calls in a day. The experience is inconsistent for the lead and the results are impossible to learn from.

A cadence gives you three things:

- **Consistency.** Every lead gets the same fair set of attempts.
- **Measurability.** You can see which touch produces the booking and trim the rest.
- **Boundaries.** There is a defined end, which protects both your brand and the lead.

If speed matters most in the first minutes after an enquiry, read [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead-ai-calling) alongside this article.

## Principles to design around

**Front-load, then widen.** Interest decays. Make most of your effort in the first day or two, then space attempts out.

**Vary the channel.** Repeating the same type of touch to someone who did not respond is less effective than offering another route. A person who ignores calls may reply to a text, and the reverse.

**Vary the time.** If the first call was mid-morning, try the afternoon next. [Best time to call leads](/blog/best-time-to-call-leads) has the detail.

**Give each touch a reason.** "Just checking in" adds nothing. Each message should offer something: a slot, an answer, a useful link.

**Have a ceiling.** Decide the maximum number of attempts in advance and honour it.

## A seven-touch example

This is an illustration for a warm lead who has just requested contact. Adjust it to your market, your volume and the rules where you operate.

| Touch | When | Channel | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Within minutes | Call | Reach them while the enquiry is fresh |
| 2 | Same day, a few hours later | SMS | "Tried to call, here is a way to book" |
| 3 | Next morning | Call | Second live attempt at a different hour |
| 4 | Day 2 | Email | Answer the likely question, offer a slot |
| 5 | Day 4 | Call | Third attempt, different time of day |
| 6 | Day 7 | SMS | Short check-in with a clear yes/no |
| 7 | Day 10 to 14 | Call or email | Final attempt, says this is the last one |

If any touch gets a conversation, the cadence pauses and a different path takes over: book, qualify, or close out.

### Notes on the example

- **Touch 1 is a call because it is the most likely to produce a real conversation while interest is high.**
- **The text is not a pitch.** Something like "Hi Priya, it's Harbour Dental. We tried you about your whitening enquiry. Reply with a good time or book here" is enough. Check that you have permission to text, since the rules on marketing messages are separate from the rules on calls.
- **The final touch tells them it is the last.** That is both courteous and more likely to get a reply than another neutral nudge.

## Different cadences for different leads

One cadence does not suit every lead. Segment by warmth:

- **Hot (asked to be contacted now).** Dense and fast in the first day, up to seven or eight touches over two weeks.
- **Warm (downloaded or registered, but did not ask for a call).** Fewer calls, more email, over a longer period.
- **Cold (list or purchased data).** Few attempts, widely spaced, and a clear reason in every message. Pay close attention to consent and local rules.
- **Dormant (old leads).** One short sequence; see [reactivate old leads with AI calls](/blog/reactivate-old-leads-with-ai-calls).

## Knowing when to stop

Stop the cadence immediately when:

- The lead books or buys.
- The lead says no, or uses clear language like "not interested".
- The lead asks to be removed or says stop calling. Treat this as a hard stop and add them to your suppression list; see [do not call lists and dialer hygiene](/blog/do-not-call-lists-and-dialer-hygiene).
- The planned touches have run out.

After the end, do not simply abandon the lead. Move them to a slower nurture list, such as a monthly email, and revisit when something changes. This article contains general information, not legal advice: marketing, consent and calling rules vary, so check the current requirements for where you operate.

## Reviewing the cadence

After a few weeks, look at which touches produce outcomes:

- Which touch gets the first conversation? If it is nearly always the first call, the later touches may be adding little.
- How many leads are still not reached after the last touch? A large share suggests data or hours problems more than a cadence problem.
- Are leads complaining? If so, you are probably calling too much or at poor times.

Trim touches that never work, add spacing where people complain, and keep each change small. [AI calling metrics that matter](/blog/ai-calling-metrics-that-matter) lists the numbers to watch.
