AI calling for recruiters is most useful at the first screen, where the work is repetitive and the questions are factual. An AI voice agent calls each applicant, confirms interest, asks the same set of questions about availability, location and notice period, and books those who fit into a recruiter's calendar. A human still reviews every summary and makes every decision. Used that way, it gives recruiters their mornings back without turning hiring into an automated lottery.

## The first-screen bottleneck

A job advert can bring in dozens or hundreds of applications. Recruiters do the same few things over and over:

- Check the CV against the basic requirements.
- Phone the candidate to confirm they are interested.
- Ask about availability, location, salary and notice period.
- Arrange a time for a proper interview.

Much of this is phone tag. Candidates are at work during the day, miss calls and reply days later. Recruiters lose time, and good candidates drift to the agency that called first.

## What an AI screening call looks like

A call might run three to five minutes:

1. The agent says it is an AI assistant calling on behalf of the agency about the role the candidate applied for.
2. It confirms that the candidate is still interested and that it is a good time to talk.
3. It asks a fixed list of questions.
4. It answers basic questions about the role from the knowledge base.
5. It offers interview slots from the recruiter's Google Calendar, or notes that a recruiter will call.
6. It produces a summary and transcript for the recruiter.

Here is how a short exchange could go:

> Agent: Hi Priya, this is an AI assistant calling for Calder Recruitment about the warehouse supervisor role you applied for. Is now a good time for a few quick questions?
>
> Candidate: Yes, go ahead.
>
> Agent: Thanks. First, are you still looking for work, and are you able to work on site in Leeds?

## Questions that belong in a screening call

Stick to factual, job-relevant questions that you ask every candidate in the same way.

| Topic | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| Interest | Are you still looking for a role like this? |
| Location | Can you travel to the site, and what is your commute? |
| Availability | When could you start, and what is your notice period? |
| Right to work | Do you have the right to work in this country? (where lawful to ask) |
| Pay | What range are you looking for? |
| Core requirement | Do you hold the licence or certification listed in the advert? |

Avoid anything that touches protected characteristics, such as age, health, family plans or religion. Keep the agent to the questions you give it, and tell it to politely decline to discuss anything else.

For more on how to design questions and read the answers, see [AI lead qualification calls](/blog/ai-lead-qualification-calls). The principles carry over from sales to hiring.

## Humans make the decisions

This is the most important design rule. The AI collects and schedules. People decide.

- Do not auto-reject candidates based on a call.
- Do not rely on tone, accent or speech patterns. These are unreliable and can be discriminatory.
- Use summaries as a starting point. Recruiters should be able to read the transcript.
- Review a sample of calls weekly to check that every candidate is treated consistently.

In some jurisdictions, automated decision-making in hiring is regulated, and some places require notice or audits. This is general information, not legal advice, so check the rules where you recruit.

## Handling real candidate behaviour

Candidates do not behave like a spreadsheet. Plan for:

- **"Can you call back later?"** The agent should offer a time and honour it with a retry, or let the candidate book a slot.
- **Questions about salary or the client.** Only share what you have approved. For anything else, offer a recruiter call.
- **Candidates who dislike talking to AI.** Offer a human callback immediately, with no friction.
- **Nervous or emotional candidates.** The agent should stay calm, brief and kind. If someone is upset, hand off.

An objection library helps. See [handling objections with an AI voice agent](/blog/handling-objections-with-ai-voice-agent) for how to build it, and adapt the examples for candidates.

## Client-side use: calling to confirm interviews

The same tool can call candidates the day before an interview to confirm attendance and the address, which helps clients who complain about no-shows. A call from the agency, offering to reschedule if needed, is more effective than an email many candidates will not read.

## Measuring the benefit

Track:

- Candidates reached out of those called.
- Candidates who completed the screen.
- Interviews booked.
- Interview attendance.
- Recruiter time per placement.

If completion is low, check whether calling hours or call length are the problem. The summary and transcript pages in the dashboard show where candidates hang up.

## Limits to be honest about

- Speech recognition struggles with some accents and noisy environments, so a fixed script can disadvantage some candidates. Offer text or a human call as an alternative.
- The agent cannot judge cultural fit or soft skills and should not try.
- Specialist or senior roles often need a human from the first call.
- You are responsible for fairness and legal compliance in each market you recruit in.

## Keeping the candidate experience good

Candidates remember how an agency treated them, and many are also future clients. Tell candidates how long the call will take, say what happens next, and keep that promise. If a role has been filled, have the agent say so honestly rather than collecting answers that will go nowhere. Give each candidate a way to ask for a recruiter, and make sure someone actually calls back within the time you stated. A short, respectful call that ends with a clear next step is better for your brand than a long one that leaves people guessing.
