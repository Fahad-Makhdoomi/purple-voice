A warm transfer from an AI to a human is a live handoff where the person who picks up already knows who the caller is and what they want. Done well, the caller never repeats themselves. Done badly, it is a cold transfer with extra waiting, and it undoes whatever goodwill the agent built.

The idea behind AI calling is not to replace the closer but to get the right calls to them faster and better prepared. This article sets out when to hand off, what the agent should say, what the human should see, and how to cope when the handoff fails. For the wider question of dividing work, [AI voice agent vs human SDR](/blog/ai-voice-agent-vs-human-sdr) is a good companion.

## Why handoff design matters

Most of the value in a transfer is lost or kept in about ten seconds. If the human picks up and says "Hi, who is this and what is it about?", the caller concludes that the previous conversation was wasted. If they pick up and say "Hi Priya, I hear you're looking at a three-bedroom renovation and you'd like to talk about timing", the caller feels looked after.

That difference does not depend on technology. It depends on two design decisions: which calls get transferred, and what the human knows when they pick up.

## When to hand off

Write the transfer triggers into the agent's brief. Four groups cover most cases.

### The caller is ready to act

The caller has said they want a quote, a price commitment, a visit or to proceed. This is the main reason to transfer: a person can close or commit in ways the agent should not. Define "ready" with concrete signals, such as the caller asking for a start date, agreeing to a price range, or saying "let's do it".

### The caller asks for a person

If someone asks for a human, give them one. Do not try to talk them out of it. A clear request for a person is a strong reason, and respecting it builds more trust than any answer the agent can give.

### The topic is beyond the agent's remit

Complaints, contract terms, legal questions, medical or financial advice, and negotiation of price all belong with a person. Tell the agent the list of topics to hand over rather than answer.

### The caller is upset or the call is going badly

If the tone turns frustrated, the agent should apologise and offer a person rather than push on. A transfer here is damage control, and may be better as a callback if no one is free.

## What the agent should say

The agent's job at the moment of transfer is to set expectations and keep the caller comfortable. Three short lines usually do it:

1. **Explain what is happening.** "I'm going to bring in one of the team who can go through pricing with you."
2. **Confirm what they know.** "I've told them you're looking at a loft conversion and would like to start in the spring."
3. **Set a short expectation.** "It'll take a few seconds to connect. Is that alright?"

Illustrative exchange:

> **Agent:** "That sounds like a good fit for a site visit. I'll bring in Sam from the surveying team, who can confirm dates and pricing. I've let him know you're looking at a loft conversion for the spring. One moment while I connect you."

Avoid long hold music, vague promises ("someone will be right with you"), or leaving silence. If the connection takes more than a few seconds, the agent should check in.

## What the human needs to see

Even if the agent briefs the caller aloud, the person receiving the call benefits from a short summary. Ideally they see, before or as they pick up:

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Name and number | Avoids "who am I speaking to?" |
| Reason for the original call | Gives context to the enquiry |
| What they said they want | Lets the human start in the right place |
| Key facts collected | Budget, timeline, property type, whatever you qualify on |
| Objections or concerns raised | Avoids walking into them blind |
| Reason for transfer | Ready to buy, asked for a person, complaint |

## Making sure someone is there

A warm transfer only works if a person can answer. Before launching:

- **Staff the transfer number** during the same hours the campaign runs. Calling outside staffed hours produces failed handoffs.
- **Decide who gets the call.** One closer, a rota, or a team line.
- **Test the transfer** by running a test call to your phone and having a colleague take the handoff. Check the audio, the delay and whether the person hears what they need.

## When the transfer fails

It will sometimes. The agent needs a plan, written into its brief:

- **No answer:** "I'm sorry, nobody's free at the moment. Can I book a time for Sam to call you? Would this afternoon or tomorrow morning suit you better?" Then book it, using the calendar integration if you have one.
- **Line busy or dropped:** same approach, with an apology.
- **Caller refuses to wait:** offer a callback and end the call politely.

The worst failure is silence or a promise that is not kept. If the transfer cannot be completed, the agent should say so clearly and offer the next best thing. Booking logic is covered in [AI appointment setting](/blog/ai-appointment-setting).

## Common mistakes

- **Transferring too early.** If the caller has barely said anything, the human gets a conversation with nothing in it.
- **Transferring too late.** The agent keeps pushing after the caller wanted a person.
- **No briefing.** The human asks the caller to repeat everything.
- **Handoff to an unstaffed number.** Calls fail and the caller is worse off than before.
- **Forgetting disclosure.** If the caller asks whether they are speaking to an AI, answer honestly before and after the transfer. See [disclose AI on calls](/blog/disclose-ai-on-calls).

## Measuring the handoff

Track how many calls are transferred, how many connect, and what happens next. Look at:

- The share of transferred calls that turn into a booked meeting or sale.
- The share of transfers that fail to connect.
- How often the human reports having had to ask basic questions again.

Review transcripts of handoffs in particular, since this is where a weak brief shows. The numbers worth watching are covered in [AI calling metrics that matter](/blog/ai-calling-metrics-that-matter).
